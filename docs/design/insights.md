# Insights

The one screen where charts belong, and the only place they are allowed. Every figure draws in **ink at graded opacity** over a `plate-2` well, never in amber: a chart wants a series colour, and if the series takes amber then this is the one screen where the accent means "data" instead of "act". Three things are exempt, and nothing else. A lit day is amber wherever it is drawn, in the thirty-day strip and in the day grid, because those are the streak’s own material. Today’s bar on Ahead is amber because today is the day the learner can act on, which is what the accent means. The Recall endpoint is an amber dot because it marks which of the buckets the end label is talking about. Everything else is ink, including that end label’s value: amber marks what to act on, never what a number says. Amber in particular never marks the heaviest day ahead, which is named in the sentence under the chart instead, where it can be said in words rather than competing with today for the accent.

Four plates in a 2x2 grid — Recall, Consistency, Cards, Ahead — each carrying a number and one line of plain words. The line is not a caption. 91% says nothing until the plate says the schedule aims for 90, and a grid of numbers without those lines is the dashboard the product does without. Under them the day grid runs full width, then the cards that keep slipping.

Consistency and the day grid answer different questions and neither replaces the other. The thirty-day strip is texture: which days, and how long the runs were, drawn lit or unlit and never graded, because over a stretch that short a scoreboard makes a heavy Tuesday look better than a steady week. The grid is the long view and the one figure on the screen that does grade a day, because the question it answers is how much, not whether.

The grid is a column a week and a row a weekday, Monday first, every row named in the locale's narrow weekday and standing outside the scroller so the names hold still while the months move under them. One continuous field rather than a block per month, so the weekday rows run the whole way across and a month is marked by its short name alone; a gap or a rule between months would cut the one thing the shape is for. It spans whole weeks, so the field is a rectangle whatever weekday a month begins on, which means the first column reaches into the month before and the last runs to the end of this week. It scrolls sideways and comes to rest at the start of a month, which is also the step the arrows take, and it opens on today.

The field is always at least the last year, however short the history, and longer when the history is longer. The constant frame is the same rule the strip follows for the same reason: a field cut to the days that exist is mostly empty space beside a first week, and it says nothing about how much of the year the habit covered.

A day carries the seven lights' three steps of amber against its own goal, so lowering today's goal never rewrites how an earlier day looks; a day from before goals, or one an import filled in, is drawn against the goal in force now, which is the only reference it has. A day that counted toward the streak without filling — nothing left to do, or nothing due — carries an amber ring rather than a full cell. A day still to come is a dashed hairline, which is this system's mark for "not here yet", so it can never read as a day that was missed. Imported history shades a day and never rings it: it lights the day it landed on and was never measured against a goal, so the streak's own count of a day and the grid's differ by design.

The section is headed **Day by day** — "Activity" already names the audit screen — with every review the history holds counted beside it, not the span on screen, which moves as you page. A day says what it held in a tooltip, which is the one figure on Insights that answers by pointer rather than in the plate's own line; the same sentence is each cell's accessible name, so a reader gets it without the popup.

`RunStrip` joins consecutive lit days into one capsule. That is the whole idea: a row of separate marks has to be counted, where an unbroken capsule is a run whose length you can see.

Every figure is drawn inside a constant frame rather than scaled to the data that exists. The strip is always thirty days and the grid always at least a year, so a learner's first day reads as one day instead of filling the plate the way a finished month would. Days before the first review are unlit, not absent.

Recall stops being a line when there is not enough of it to be one. Below three buckets it draws the tally instead: one mark per graded review, the forgotten ones in the heavier ink. A rate hides its sample size, and the sample size is the whole reason an early figure swings — the picture says what the sentence under it already says. The period switcher follows the same rule and offers only the spans the history can tell apart, because three buttons returning one number read as a broken control.

The chart's reference rule is inset to the series and its label sits in a gutter beside the plot, never on the marks. Each bucket carries a dot, so four weekly measurements cannot be mistaken for a daily trend. The end labels name the span the line covers and the value its last bucket reached, which is the number the headline figure is not: the headline is the whole period, the endpoint is the last bucket alone.

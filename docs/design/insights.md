# Insights

The one screen where charts belong, and the only place they are allowed. Every figure draws in **ink at graded opacity** over a `plate-2` well, never in amber: a chart wants a series colour, and if the series takes amber then this is the one screen where the accent means "data" instead of "act". The two exceptions are the lights, which are the streak, and the single bar the sentence above the chart is pointing at.

Four plates in a 2x2 grid — Recall, Consistency, Cards, Ahead — each carrying a number and one line of plain words. The line is not a caption. 91% says nothing until the plate says the schedule aims for 90, and a grid of numbers without those lines is the dashboard the product does without. Under them the month bars run full width, then the cards that keep slipping.

Consistency and the months answer different questions and neither replaces the other. The thirty-day strip is texture: which days, and how long the runs were. The month bars are trend: whether the habit is holding across seasons. Both draw a day as lit or unlit and never grade it by how many cards it held, because grading turns a habit picture into a scoreboard and makes a heavy Tuesday look better than a steady one.

`RunStrip` joins consecutive lit days into one capsule. That is the whole idea: a row of separate marks has to be counted, where an unbroken capsule is a run whose length you can see.

Every figure is drawn inside a constant frame rather than scaled to the data that exists. The strip is always thirty days and a month bar is always its calendar month, so a learner's first day reads as one day instead of filling the plate the way a finished month would. Days before the first review are unlit, not absent.

Recall stops being a line when there is not enough of it to be one. Below three buckets it draws the tally instead: one mark per graded review, the forgotten ones in the heavier ink. A rate hides its sample size, and the sample size is the whole reason an early figure swings — the picture says what the sentence under it already says. The period switcher follows the same rule and offers only the spans the history can tell apart, because three buttons returning one number read as a broken control.

The chart's reference rule is inset to the series and its label sits in a gutter beside the plot, never on the marks. Each bucket carries a dot, so four weekly measurements cannot be mistaken for a daily trend. The end labels name the span the line covers and the value its last bucket reached, which is the number the headline figure is not: the headline is the whole period, the endpoint is the last bucket alone.

# Japanese

A beginner learning Japanese should find that Lymi just works: kanji arrive with readings, readings fade as words are learned, audio says the word right, and a deck from Anki arrives whole. There is no Japanese mode and no setting. Not decided: the order of slices 4 to 6.

## Evidence

Kanji carry meaning and are read differently from word to word (日 in 日本, 毎日, 今日), so textbooks print furigana, small hiragana over the kanji, until the reader knows them. Before this work, the web review showed the reading before the reveal, audio spoke the kanji, the duplicate key ignored the reading, and readings were a line of text. A test import of Kaishi 1.5k skipped all 2,999 native recordings, dropped pitch accent and furigana in examples, and merged the sentence translation and a frequency number into the notes.

## Decision

- **Fields.** No new ones. The term is written as natives write it, in dictionary form. Its reading stays in the pronunciation field in hiragana, and furigana is derived by aligning the two. Examples carry readings inline in Anki's notation, `食[た]べます`; plain-text uses strip the brackets. CONTEXT.md names this a **Reading**.
- **Furigana.** Over the term on the cue while the card is New or Learning, gone once it is Known; always over the term in the answer. Over an example's kanji except where the kanji and reading match one of the learner's Known cards.
- **The AI.** It writes example readings, and adds them to a learner's example only when the text without brackets is unchanged.
- **Audio.** Spoken from the reading. A recorded clip from an import is the card's audio and is never replaced; generated speech fills only cards without one. Sentence audio waits.
- **Import.** Pitch accent and the sentence translation each become one notes line; a frequency number is dropped.
- **Left out.** Romaji as a reading aid, extra review modes, kanji cards as a type, handwriting and typed answers.

## Delivery

1. Correctness: the reading after the reveal, audio from the reading, duplicates, the AI's checks, a Japanese font, Smart add readings.
2. Furigana on the web, with the shared code in `packages/core`.
3. Import fidelity: recorded audio, pitch accent, notes.
4. Furigana on the phone.
5. Example furigana that fades over known words, on the web and the phone. The Known pairs come from every deck's card list the clients already keep for offline use, with the device's own grades laid over them, so it costs no request and a Forgot brings a reading back at once; a reading that fades keeps its room. The card form's preview shows every reading, because it is where the learner checks what they typed.
6. A beginner's start, built on the published Read Japanese Kana deck rather than new Hiragana and Katakana decks: Welcome offers a Japanese learner Read Japanese Kana, then JLPT N5, JLPT N4 and Japanese Essentials. The page at `lymi.app/languages/japanese` waits for a design.

## Japanese audio: a listening decision

The learner should hear the reading their card teaches, with natural Japanese delivery and the intended lexical accent. Reading correctness comes before voice preference. A term recording from an import remains authoritative; generated speech fills only a card without one. The first implementation keeps the current Hiroki voice and adds preceding-only context; alternative voice selection and example audio remain separate decisions.

The [listening comparison](https://lymi-japanese-voice-lab.neatgrape8.chatgpt.site) keeps a common kana-only test separate from an accent-guidance experiment. Its 19 cases cover 上手 as じょうず and うわて, 今日 as きょう and こんにち, 一日 as いちにち and ついたち, 箸/橋/端, 雨/飴, 紙/髪/神, three はしが particle checks, and two example sentences. Each clip retains its exact input, model, voice, guidance, usage and hash. Ratings and preferences stay in the listener's browser and can be exported; no API credentials or learner content are published.

The candidates are ElevenLabs Hiroki, Hinata and Shizuka on `eleven_v4`, Gemini 3.1 Flash TTS Preview with Kore, Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS with Kore, and Azure's `ja-JP-NanamiNeural` and `ja-JP-KeitaNeural`. Azure remains deferred. The common test uses the same kana; the input experiment holds the voice fixed. ElevenLabs compares kana, written Japanese and experimental IPA tone letters; Gemini 3.1 compares kana, silent kanji/meaning and an explicit accent number. This tests input strategy separately from voice preference.

Kana-identical homophones give a kana-only request identical information; variation between takes does not prove semantic understanding. A following particle helps expose the difference between a final-mora accent and an unaccented word, which isolated audio can hide. Some homophones legitimately share an accent. The annotated accent targets in this small test still need native Japanese confirmation before they become a release test set.

The choice gate is Kateryna's listening preference plus native Japanese verification of reading, lexical accent, mora length, gemination and naturalness. A pleasant voice, a general TTS leaderboard, a successful API response, or a speech recognizer's transcript does not establish these. Repeat the difficult cases and check a held-out set before changing the default; one take per case does not establish reliability. Tokyo Japanese is the proposed reference, with legitimate variants treated as variants rather than automatic errors.

At the fetched `main` used for this shape, `speech.ts` prefers Hiroki for Japanese, then Gemini 3.1/Kore when its API key exists, OpenAI and Chirp as configured. `SpeechRequest` carries text and language, and the audio service speaks `kanaReading(card) ?? card.term`. [PR #830](https://github.com/krambertech/lymi/pull/830) was closed without merge when checked on 18 October 2026; its kanji-context change must not be assumed present on `main`.

### Proposed example audio

Add a separate play control beside the example when the answer is revealed, on the web and phone. An example never starts automatically, never reveals itself through audio before recall, and never borrows the term's speaker control. The first slice is authenticated playback only; public generation and advance generation are excluded. A cached clip may play offline when the existing client audio cache already holds it; first-time synthesis waits for a connection.

Use `parseReadings()` in `packages/core/src/reading.ts` as the shared parser and join each segment's reading when present, otherwise its written text. `毎朝[まいあさ]パンを食[た]べます。` becomes `まいあさパンをたべます。`; do not merely strip the readings, speak both forms, or read the brackets. Preserve punctuation and Japanese particles in sentence context. If a kanji span lacks a reading, generation remains an inference; playback does not silently enrich or edit the example. Keep the written Japanese separately as silent context for providers that support it.

Generate only on the first explicit press for the current example. Replays use R2. Do not generate during card creation, import, enrichment, reveal, scrolling or catalogue warming. Coalesce simultaneous requests for the same input fingerprint so repeated presses or two devices do not buy duplicate clips. A cached response does not consume a generation allowance. A client leaving the card must not start playback or commit a stale pointer when the request settles.

Extend the owning `apps/web/src/server/services/audio.ts` path rather than adding a second speech stack. Use a separate example namespace under the card, with an explicit kind in the cache identity. Fingerprint the exact written example, resolved spoken text, language/locale, permitted kanji context, validated accent guidance where applicable, provider, model, voice and prompt-policy version. If meaning is used as prompt context it belongs in the fingerprint too. Do not put private text into R2 keys or logs. Keep term and example invalidation independent.

The proposed first limit is 500 Unicode characters of resolved speech, one synthesis in flight per card/kind/fingerprint, at most 100 new example clips per owner per day, a 30-second provider deadline and the existing 8 MB audio response cap. The existing example field can remain 2,000 characters; an example beyond the speech limit is retained without truncating it into a misleading clip. Count shared-deck generation against the owner and deployment-wide provider budget as well. Reserve the allowance atomically before the provider request; do not automatically retry an ambiguous timeout that may already be billed. These limits remain proposals to accept alongside the feature.

Fall back only after a definite provider failure, never after an R2 write failure. Store audio and metadata before remembering its key, and compare the card's current input fingerprint and source before committing; an example or reading edit during generation must not attach stale audio. Keep a successful cached example stable until a relevant input or policy changes. If a preferred provider retry is later wanted, give it a bounded cooldown rather than paying for it on every playback. Return a normal retryable error when no clip exists, with no fabricated or term-audio substitute.

Imported recordings remain separate from generated term audio and example audio. Provider changes, prompt changes, cache versions and example edits never overwrite them. The existing rule that a new term detaches its recording still applies; the stored recorded clip itself is not rewritten. Any new generation outcome belongs to the existing audit service and the owner of the card.

### Can imported accent notes steer the voice?

Potentially, after validation and a provider-specific adapter; not by passing the notes text through as spoken input. `docs/data-model.md` describes an imported downstep line such as `はꜜし [1]`. Match its folded reading to the card's reading, count morae with the existing `moraCount()`, accept only a valid position from zero through the mora count, and require downstep and numeric notation to agree. Treat zero as unaccented, not a drop before the first mora. Multiple conflicting patterns, a mismatched reading, malformed input and unrelated free-form notes do not become instructions. Preserve the original notes unchanged.

Gemini's prompts can express the written term, reading and accent position. The comparison contains controlled Gemini 3.1 variants to hear whether that guidance helps; prompt acceptance is not a guarantee of the resulting pitch. Gemini 3.8 separates delivery instructions into structured speech metadata, so it needs its own adapter and cache policy rather than an unexamined model-name swap. [Gemini TTS documentation](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/speech-generation).

Eleven v4 documents inline IPA between slashes and pronunciation-dictionary phoneme rules, including non-English pronunciation; it does not support SSML phoneme tags. That establishes a pronunciation-control surface, not a guarantee that Japanese lexical downstep is obeyed. Kana plus `[1]` is not itself IPA, and replacing pitch accent with IPA stress would be wrong. A validated conversion and a listening test of the precise Hiroki/v4 configuration are required before using imported notes this way. [ElevenLabs dictionary guide](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/pronunciation-dictionary-guide), [non-English dictionary support](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-api/guides/how-to/text-to-speech/pronunciation-dictionaries).

Azure supports SSML aliases and phonemes for supported voices/locales, but global `<prosody pitch>` is not a word's lexical accent. Validate the Japanese phone alphabet and the chosen neural voice before defining an accent-position conversion; the existence of SSML alone does not prove it. Keep phoneme XML server-generated and escape the card text. [Azure pronunciation SSML](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/speech-synthesis-markup-pronunciation).

Do not transfer a dictionary-form term accent blindly onto an inflected or compounded example span. Sentence audio first uses the example's own inline readings and natural sentence context. Reliable span-level accent guidance is a later, independently verified slice.

### Listening reference and specialist challengers

Kateryna prefers ElevenLabs' sound but has not selected a final voice. The next audition therefore prioritises Hiroki, Hinata and Shizuka on v4; Hinata and Shizuka come from ElevenLabs' curated Japanese v4 collection, whose labels are discovery aids rather than independent correctness evidence. The comparison retains the earlier baseline, written-input and IPA experiments. Each ElevenLabs voice has the same 19 kana inputs, 19 written inputs and 11 experimental IPA inputs. Written input deliberately allows the model to infer a reading; identical kanji such as 上手 are not enough to distinguish the requested readings. The separate Gemini experiments remain available.

The listening page starts with a human reference lesson and schematic high/low patterns before any rating. [Yukitty's own recorded lesson](https://yukitty-jp.com/vocabulary/pitch-accent-30/) includes the あめ, かみ and はし contrasts; it is embedded from the original YouTube publication without copying the recordings. [TUFS](https://www.coelang.tufs.ac.jp/ja/en/pmod/practical/01-08-01.php) explains the distinction and following-particle test; [University of Tokyo teaching notes](https://ocw.u-tokyo.ac.jp/lecture_files/engin_01/8/notes/ja/H1-TextToSpeech.pdf) give the three はし patterns. [OJAD](https://www.gavo.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ojad/) is useful for accent patterns and human verb/adjective recordings, but provides no noun recordings for this set. Sentence prosody predictions and any synthetic demonstration must not be labelled human references or ground truth. Native review still checks each release sample and held-out cases.

The ElevenLabs experiment wraps IPA segmental transcription and high/low tone letters in slashes. This is a hypothesis to test, not a documented Japanese accent-position API or a validated conversion of imported notes. [ElevenLabs' best-practice guide](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/text-to-speech/best-practices) warns that pronunciation results vary by voice and phrase. A successful synthesis or a visible target pattern says nothing about whether the generated pitch follows it; listeners may mark “Need a native speaker” without selecting a winner.

Aivis Cloud is the proposed Japanese-specialist challenger to audition after ElevenLabs. Its [API](https://api.aivis-project.com/v1/docs) exposes dictionary katakana readings and Tokyo `accent_type`, with zero meaning unaccented, and synthesis selects a dictionary UUID. The [current tariff](https://aivis-project.com/cloud-api/) is ¥440 per 10,000 characters including tax, or ¥44,000 per million; signup includes ¥500 credit. The chosen voice/model must permit commercial app playback. Its separate per-request g2p editing API remains in development, so concurrent per-card generation must use immutable/versioned dictionaries instead of overwriting a shared dictionary. Documented controls and vendor quality claims do not establish a listening winner; no Aivis clips have been generated.

[Amazon Polly](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/en_en/polly/latest/dg/ph-table-japanese.html) is a useful hosted control baseline because `x-amazon-pron-kana` explicitly encodes Japanese pitch accent, unlike reading-only yomigana. Its voice/engine combination, sound, account access and cost still need a trial. [VOICEVOX](https://github.com/VOICEVOX/voicevox_engine) provides direct accent-phrase and per-mora pitch editing for a local synthetic demonstration, with compute, attribution and voice-specific terms. Local AivisSpeech is not a drop-in pitch editor: [its compatibility contract](https://github.com/Aivis-Project/AivisSpeech-Engine#voicevox-api-%E3%81%A8%E3%81%AE%E4%BA%92%E6%8F%9B%E6%80%A7%E3%81%AB%E3%81%A4%E3%81%84%E3%81%A6) says individual mora pitch values are ignored. CoeFont and AITalk are later auditions because API plans or sales requirements add overhead to this small test.

[Cloudflare AI Gateway supports ElevenLabs natively](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-gateway/usage/providers/elevenlabs/), using the existing ElevenLabs token and allowance. Aivis' native synthesis API could plausibly use a [Custom Provider](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai-gateway/configuration/custom-providers/); this is an integration inference until credential forwarding, binary audio/streaming and failures are tested. Gateway routing does not add pronunciation control or run a local engine. Evaluation calls use the isolated provider scripts; no production Gateway settings have changed.

Japanese-specific evaluation resources now exist: [Joyo Kanji Yomi Benchmark](https://github.com/sbintuitions/Joyo-Kanji-Yomi-Benchmark) measures kanji-reading errors, while [J-HARD-TTS-Eval](https://github.com/Parakeet-Inc/J-HARD-TTS-Eval) studies sequence robustness. These can extend a held-out reading test; their metrics do not establish correct lexical pitch or a best voice for Lymi.

### Sentence context while preserving the requested reading

Kateryna reports that the earlier IPA experiment diverged from the human reference and that written Japanese can select an entirely different reading. Neither experiment establishes a reliable accent adapter. Keep the requested kana fixed when testing semantic context rather than choosing raw kanji as the production speech source.

ElevenLabs' [Create speech API](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/api-reference/text-to-speech/convert) accepts separate `previous_text` and `next_text` for speech continuity; these are not documented as arbitrary instructions or guaranteed lexical-accent controls. The new pilot compares the same kana alone against kana with natural Japanese sentence fragments for 上手 in both readings, 雨/飴 and the three はしが meanings, across Hiroki, Hinata and Shizuka. The 21 new clips are API-success evidence only. For the bridge case, the requested speech is はしが, previous text is 川を渡るための and next text is 見えます。; the English gloss on the listening page is explanatory and is not sent as input.

A context-dependent utterance can have different phrase prosody from a dictionary citation. Compare it with a matching human utterance before declaring the pitch right or wrong; a dictionary diagram is not the required entire contour of every sentence. For future example audio, speak the complete example with its own resolved readings and verify natural delivery. Do not place free-form guidance into spoken text and assume it will stay silent. Any accepted context strategy must include both context fields in cache identity, maintain reading checks and pass repeated native-reviewed cases. The listening page defaults to the two-way context experiment and retains the earlier written/IPA experiment separately; production remains unchanged.

Kateryna reports that the before/after-context clips cut abruptly toward a following word. The current audition therefore omits `next_text` entirely, retaining the same requested kana and `previous_text`. The preceding-only pilot adds 21 clips (the same 7 cases in 3 voices) and becomes the default A/B comparison. The previous before/after clips and their ratings remain separate for comparison. Request inspection confirms the field is absent, but whether this resolves the audible ending still needs listening; do not claim the problem fixed from an API response or duration alone. Any eventual production context policy should omit following text unless separately justified and verified.

### Short generated lead-ins

Kateryna accepts the preceding-only approach for the next audition: she hears the intended contrasts and prefers it to the before/after experiment. This selects an input strategy to develop, not a final voice or a guarantee of native accuracy. Keep `next_text` absent. Keep the card's resolved kana as the only requested speech, with `previous_text` carrying a short natural fragment rather than a complete preceding sentence.

Generate the fragment from the written term, exact reading, language and intended meaning. Aim for 2–10 Japanese characters and cap it at 16; prefer the shortest grammatical fragment that still distinguishes the intended sense. Require a concrete use, action, object or setting. Generic adjectives such as “new” and “old” fail because they fit several homophones. Exclude target repetition, compounds that change the target, conjugation changes, dialogue, questions, emotion, intensity, politeness cues, speaking instructions, accent notation, markup and punctuation. Return no fragment when a useful neutral one cannot be produced. Treat card contents as data rather than instructions. These bounds reduce influence on delivery; they cannot eliminate phrase prosody or guarantee register and lexical accent.

The listening comparison holds the voice and kana fixed and compares the earlier handwritten lead-in with an AI-generated short one, for the same seven cases and three ElevenLabs voices. Its reproducibility artifact records the exact prompt, inputs, model and output. The candidate policy uses fragments such as 川にかかる before はしが for a bridge and 机の before はしが for an edge. Mechanical validation checks length, forbidden characters and target repetition; semantic and native listening review remain necessary. Do not automatically accept every model output solely because it passes a JSON schema.

Persist the validated fragment, including a no-fragment result, independently of the generated audio and voice. Key it by the written term, resolved reading, language, meaning and context-policy version, with no private text in keys or logs. A server-owned JSON artifact under the existing `cards/{id}/` R2 namespace can hold the result and provenance without adding a learner-facing field; card cleanup already owns that prefix. Store the fragment before synthesis. Reuse it across voice/provider changes and include its actual value and policy version in the audio fingerprint. A meaning, reading or term edit invalidates the context and affected generated term audio. Conditional attachment must compare this complete input identity, not only the term and language.

For newly enriched cards, include the fragment in the existing enrichment result where the required reading and meaning are available, avoiding a separate language-model round trip. For existing or imported cards without generated context, the first explicit audio press may make one additional context request before TTS; later plays use the stored context and R2 clip. This is proposed behavior, not implemented behavior. Coalesce simultaneous context misses before the provider loop. Bound invalid-output repair to one additional attempt; an ambiguous timeout must not trigger an immediate duplicate request. On failure, preserve the reading-only fallback, store a bounded retry cooldown separately from a valid no-fragment result, and retain metadata so a transient context failure does not permanently count as a successful contextual clip. Imported recordings bypass context generation and synthesis entirely.

Apply this policy to Japanese isolated terms first. ElevenLabs documents continuity fields without a Japanese restriction, so a similar strategy may help ambiguous words in other languages; that is a hypothesis requiring a separate language-specific test. Do not enable it globally from Japanese evidence. Whole example sentences already provide their own semantic context and should be spoken as complete sentences from their inline readings, without an invented lead-in. Example audio remains a separate proposed slice.

### Accepted first implementation

Use the current Hiroki voice and preceding-only input policy for Japanese terms, with a dedicated Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite call on first audio generation, independent of enrichment. Kateryna accepts the small difference between the earlier handwritten and generated fragments and prioritises low cost. The first slice caches context independently of voice, pins a cheap model with a small reasoning allowance, makes one attempt, and uses a 24-hour failure cooldown. It does not add context to the expensive enrichment call or regenerate context on replay. The owning [stack decision](../stack.md#ai-enrichment-plus-lazy-multilingual-speech) specifies current behavior and limits; example audio, other-language context and accent-note adapters remain proposals. Landing regeneration is a separate delivery step.
